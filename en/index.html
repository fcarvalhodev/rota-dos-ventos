Is the Northeast's wind being thrown away?

The Northeast produces almost all of Brazil's wind power. But many plants are being ordered to shut down, companies have given up on investing and a factory closed. Here is what happened, in simple words.

📊 See the summary in icons

The short answer

Yes, some of it is being wasted. The Northeast produces more power than the lines can carry and more than the country uses at certain hours. So the grid operator orders plants to shut down. The cuts intensified in 2023, but they existed before. The industry links the problem to lower investment. The causes go back several years and several governments.

Why plants are ordered to shut down

Power has to travel along transmission lines to where people use it. The lines are like a road: when it fills up, nothing else gets through.

When there is surplus power, the ONS, which runs the national grid, orders plants to reduce output or shut down. This is called curtailment. The main reasons:

🚧Not enough lines

Many plants were built before the lines that carry power out of the Northeast.

☀️Surplus power at certain hours

At midday and on Sundays consumption falls, and rooftop solar panels, which already add up to about 50,000 MW, produce a lot.

🔌Grid reliability

After the August 2023 blackout, the ONS revised its models to better represent how plants actually behave. Safety restrictions can also require cuts.

Who is to blame?

The cuts intensified in 2023, under the Lula government, but there are earlier records. And the causes have been piling up for years. Each side points to something different:

Critics of the government say

  • Provisional Measure 1.304, from 2025, cut tax benefits for new plants and raised costs.
  • Compensation for the cuts was slow: in 2024 the plants received less than 1% of almost R$2 billion in losses, and in 2025, less than 5% of R$7 billion.
  • “We have factories in the Northeast laying people off and closing,” says the president of ABEEólica.

Defenders of the government say

  • Wind and solar are already large and no longer need the old benefits, which make electricity bills more expensive.
  • In July 2026 the government reached an agreement to pay about R$3.3 billion for the 2023–2025 cuts, without raising electricity bills.
  • The Aneel rule that made grid use more expensive for plants far from cities dates from 2022, under the previous government.

Is it true?

True

“The Northeast produces 93% of Brazil's wind power.” The most recent figure is even higher: about 95%.

Half true

“Brazil switched off an Itaipu's worth of power.” At the peak of one day, yes: 14,278 MW in the Northeast on June 28, 2026. On average, the cut is much smaller.

Half true

“The Northeast lost R$18.9 billion in wind power in 2025.” These were planned investments, not money already spent and lost. R$14.5 billion were solar projects and R$4.4 billion wind.

True

“A factory in Pernambuco closed and laid off 1,000 people.” LM Wind Power's blade factory in Suape closed in 2025. The company said it was due to lack of demand.

False

“It is all the Lula government's fault.” The cuts existed before 2023 and intensified that year; the lack of lines, the 2022 rules and the boom in rooftop panels predate it.

False

“The government did nothing.” There was a law in 2025 and a R$3.3 billion agreement in 2026 to pay for part of the cuts. The industry says it is too little and does not solve the cuts still to come.

Half true

“Flávio proposes a train linking 7 capitals.” The plan mentions a Northeast Train linking capitals, but the reports do not say how many, nor the cost or timeline.

In short

  • Almost all of Brazil's wind power comes from the Northeast.
  • There is not enough road for the power (lines), and at certain hours there is surplus power. That is why plants are ordered to shut down.
  • The industry blames the cuts for part of the drop in investment. The Pernambuco factory cited falling demand in Latin America, with no single cause demonstrated.
  • The causes come from several governments. Reports on Flávio's plan mention more lines, but do not say how many or when; their absence from the news does not prove they are absent from the full document.

What lies ahead

The ONS operating plan for 2026 foresees cuts of up to 40,000 MW at peaks. In August 2026 the ONS announced an automatic system that can cut up to 3,000 MW. The cuts also began to hit small plants connected to distributors, on June 7 and August 23, 2026.

Movimento Econômico

The 2026 agreement

Law 15.269, of November 2025, came from MP 1.304. Under it, on July 21, 2026 the government reached an agreement to pay about R$3.3 billion to wind and solar plants for cuts made from September 2023 to November 2025 due to grid problems. The amount will be deducted from about R$6 billion the plants owe the CCEE. Those who join must drop their lawsuits against the federal government. Future cuts were not included.

Agência Infra, via TAGD

Wind in Brazil

More than 33,000 MW installed and 1,103 wind farms at the end of 2024. In 2024, 3,200 new MW came online, about 90% in the Northeast. Bahia and Rio Grande do Norte together produce 65% of the country's wind power.

Other factories

GE suspended production of new wind turbines in Brazil in 2022. Siemens Gamesa stopped its Bahia factory in 2023. Aeris, a Brazilian blade maker, is renegotiating its debts.
